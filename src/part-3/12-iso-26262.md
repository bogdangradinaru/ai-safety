# 12. Functional Safety and ISO 26262

## 12.1 Introduction

**Functional safety** is the absence of unreasonable risk due to hazards caused by malfunctioning behaviour of electrical and/or electronic (E/E) systems. In road vehicles, the governing standard is **ISO 26262 — Road vehicles – Functional safety**. It was first published in 2011; the second edition (2018) has twelve parts and extends coverage to motorcycles, trucks and buses.

ISO 26262 was written for systems whose behaviour is specified by engineers and implemented in hardware and conventional software. Understanding what it does — and where learned components strain its assumptions — is the starting point for AI safety.

**Learning objectives.** After this chapter you should be able to:

- Explain the scope and the central concepts of ISO 26262.
- Perform a basic hazard analysis and risk assessment and derive an ASIL.
- Describe the safety lifecycle and the main safety artefacts.
- Explain why the standard alone is not sufficient for ML-based functions.

## 12.2 Scope and Key Concepts

ISO 26262 addresses hazards caused by **malfunctions** of E/E systems, including:

- **Random hardware faults** — e.g. a memory bit flip or a failed sensor.
- **Systematic faults** — faults in specification, design or implementation (including software bugs) which occur deterministically under certain conditions.

Basic vocabulary:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Item** | The system or function under consideration (e.g. automatic emergency braking) |
| **Hazard** | Potential source of harm caused by malfunctioning behaviour of the item |
| **Hazardous event** | Combination of a hazard and an operational situation |
| **Safety goal** | Top-level safety requirement derived from a hazardous event |
| **ASIL** | Automotive Safety Integrity Level: a risk class that determines the rigour required |
| **Safety mechanism** | Technical measure to detect or control a fault |
| **Safe state** | Operating mode without unreasonable risk |

## 12.3 The Safety Lifecycle

The standard organises activities along a lifecycle that follows the V-model:

1. **Concept phase** — item definition, hazard analysis and risk assessment (HARA), functional safety concept.
2. **System level** — technical safety concept, system design, integration, and validation.
3. **Hardware and software levels** — specification, design, implementation, and verification at each level.
4. **Production, operation, service and decommissioning.**
5. **Supporting processes** — configuration management, change management, documentation, verification, confirmation measures and independent safety assessment.

Traceability links each safety goal to its requirements, design elements and test evidence: this chain underpins the **safety case**.

## 12.4 Hazard Analysis and Risk Assessment (HARA)

For each hazardous event, three parameters are rated:

| Parameter | Classes | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Severity (S)** | S0–S3 | Potential harm: no injuries → life-threatening/fatal |
| **Exposure (E)** | E0–E4 | How often the operational situation occurs |
| **Controllability (C)** | C0–C3 | How well the driver or others can avoid harm |

The combination of S, E and C is looked up in a table that yields an **ASIL (A–D)**, with **D** the most stringent, or **QM** (quality management — no safety-specific requirements from ISO 26262). In general, the higher S, E and C, the higher the ASIL.

### Example

*Item:* automatic emergency braking (AEB).
*Hazardous event:* unintended full braking at motorway speed with a following vehicle close behind.
Severity is high (rear-end collision at speed), exposure is moderate (motorway driving is common), controllability is low (the driver cannot override instantly). The resulting ASIL would be high; the **safety goal** might read "avoid unintended full braking at speed above X".

> **Note:** HARA is performed by experts with documented rationale; ratings are judgement-based and must be justified, not merely looked up.

## 12.5 From Safety Goals to Requirements

- **Functional safety requirements** are derived from safety goals and allocated to architectural elements.
- **Technical safety requirements** refine them for the chosen system design.
- **Hardware and software safety requirements** are then derived for each component.

Each requirement inherits the ASIL of the goal it supports, unless the architecture justifies otherwise through **ASIL decomposition** — splitting a requirement across sufficiently independent elements (e.g. ASIL D → ASIL B(D) + ASIL B(D)). Decomposition is only valid with demonstrated **independence** and **freedom from interference** between elements.

## 12.6 Dealing with Faults

### Hardware

- **Failure rates and metrics.** For high ASILs the standard requires quantitative evidence, such as the Single-Point Fault Metric (SPFM), the Latent-Fault Metric (LFM) and the Probabilistic Metric for random Hardware Failures (PMHF).
- **Safety mechanisms** include redundancy, plausibility checks, watchdogs, error-correcting codes and built-in self-tests.

### Software

- Methods such as semi-formal design notations, defensive programming, requirements-based testing, structural coverage metrics (with increasing stringency by ASIL), static analysis and code review.
- Systematic faults are avoided by **process rigour**: if requirements are complete and the development follows the prescribed methods, the residual software fault rate is considered acceptable.

### Analysis techniques

Fault Tree Analysis (FTA), Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA), and dependent-failure analysis are used to argue that single and combined faults are handled.

## 12.7 Limits for Machine-Learning Components

ISO 26262 assumes that the intended behaviour can be **specified** and that a conforming implementation then behaves as specified. For a neural network:

- There is typically **no complete specification** of the function ("detect all pedestrians") from which the implementation can be derived and verified.
- The behaviour is determined by **training data** rather than by code that a reviewer can inspect; structural coverage of code says little about the learned function.
- Errors arise from **performance limitations** (e.g. missed detection in unusual lighting) rather than from faults in a hardware element or code defect.
- The established software methods and ASIL tables do not map cleanly onto learned models.

ISO 26262 is still relevant for AI-based systems: hardware executing the network, the surrounding software, and the safety mechanisms around it remain in scope, and a classical, ASIL-rated **safety monitor or fallback** is a common architectural element. But the standard does not by itself provide a way to show that the learned function is sufficiently reliable. The following chapters cover the standards that complement it: **ISO 21448 (SOTIF)** for performance limitations (Chapter 13) and **ISO/PAS 8800** for AI-specific safety engineering (Chapter 14). A related technical report, ISO/TR 5469, discusses functional safety and AI systems more generally.

## 12.8 Summary

ISO 26262 provides a rigorous, lifecycle-based framework for managing risks from E/E malfunctions: hazards are identified, classified into ASILs, and traced into requirements, design, and verification evidence. It remains essential for the hardware, software and safety mechanisms surrounding an AI component, but its assumptions of specifiable behaviour and code-level verification do not hold for learned functions — which is why complementary standards and methods are needed.
